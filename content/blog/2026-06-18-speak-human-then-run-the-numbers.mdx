Most brands don't have a marketing problem.

They have a clarity problem.

The brief is vague. The message is inconsistent. The audience is undefined. So they add more channels, more content, more spend — and wonder why none of it compounds.

Data doesn't fix that. People do.

## The order matters

There's a version of marketing that leads with the numbers. Set the KPI, reverse-engineer the funnel, optimize toward the metric. Clean. Logical. Measurable.

There's another version that leads with the story. Who are we talking to? What do they actually care about? What would make them stop scrolling, lean in, and trust us?

Both are right. The problem is when teams choose one and ignore the other.

Performance without story is noise. Every brand running the same format to the same audience with the same offer. You can optimize noise. You can't make it resonate.

Story without performance is hope. Beautiful brand, no feedback loop. You're guessing at what lands.

The sequence matters more than people admit. You have to speak human first. Then you bring in the numbers to find out what version of human is working.

## What "speak human" actually means

It doesn't mean casual. It doesn't mean conversational tone or loose grammar.

It means: your message should feel like something a person said, not something a brand committee approved.

The fastest filter: read it out loud. Does it sound like something Tim would say across a table, or does it sound like a press release?

If it sounds like a press release, start over.

Human means specific. Real brands have a point of view. They have preferences. They have things they won't do. The brands that feel generic are the ones trying to appeal to everyone, which ends up appealing to no one.

Human means honest about the trade-offs. If your product is premium, say it. If your turnaround is slow, get ahead of it. Trying to hide the edges costs you trust.

Human means the reader recognizes themselves in it. Not the aspirational version of themselves. The actual version.

## Where the numbers come in

Once you have a message that feels right, the numbers become a tool instead of a crutch.

You're not trying to find a message in the data. You're using the data to find out which version of your message lands with which people, at which moment.

That's a different job. And it's a much easier job when the message is already clear.

Good data work answers questions like: Is the same creative working with 35-year-olds and 55-year-olds, or do we need two versions? Does the emotional angle outperform the rational angle on this channel? At what frequency does performance drop?

That's signal. And it's only useful when you have something real to test against.

## The mistake I see most often

Teams treat creative and performance as competing departments. The creative team says the performance team is killing the brand. The performance team says the creative team doesn't understand how to scale.

Both are partially right.

The solution isn't a compromise. It's a better sequence.

Define the message. Make it human. Then let performance marketing find the best path to the right people at the right time.

Creative informs performance. Performance validates creative. They're not in tension. They're in sequence.

## What this looks like in practice

Before a campaign goes live, I want to be able to answer three questions without looking at a dashboard:

1. Who, specifically, is this for?
2. What do we want them to feel or understand?
3. Why would they believe us?

If the team can't answer those in a sentence each, the campaign isn't ready. Not because the creative is bad. Because the thinking isn't finished.

Once those questions have clear answers, the creative brief writes itself. And the performance team has something to optimize toward.

Speak human first. Then run the numbers.
