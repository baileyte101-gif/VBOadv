Most founders I talk to assume a bigger shop means the work is better handled. More people on the account, more coverage, fewer things falling through the cracks. It's a fair assumption. In modern terms, it's misguided.

What actually decides whether marketing works is something else. Not how many people are on the account, but how many hands a decision passes through before anything happens. Every hand-off costs you something. Context, urgency, and usually the reason the idea was good in the first place. By the time a plan has moved from a strategist to an account manager to a media buyer to whoever was free that week, it isn't quite the same plan anymore.

## The real difference is the hand-off

I spent years working inside big marketing firms before I started VBO. I can't tell you how many times I sat in a back-and-forth between team members and watched something get dropped. A note from the call that never reached the person building the thing, or a decision made twice because nobody told the second person about the first. That was just how it went, and it doesn't have to be the case anymore.

This is not a knock on the people who work at big firms. Most of them are good, and plenty of them are better at their craft than I am at mine. The problem is structural, not personal. When the person who sets the strategy isn't the person who runs it, something gets lost every time the work changes hands. Not through carelessness. Through distance.

A small, founder-led team collapses that chain to one person, start to finish. The same person sets the direction, runs the work, and sits across from you when you ask why the number moved. That's the actual case for founder-led marketing, and it has very little to do with being charming or small. It's that fewer hand-offs means less gets lost.

It's also why we run [one system instead of five vendors who never talk](/). Brand, budget, channels, creative and measurement all sit with the same person, so nothing has to be re-explained before it can get done. No re-briefing. No account manager relay. No plan that quietly changes shape between the pitch and the work.

The part people get wrong is assuming a small marketing team must be slower. That used to be true. It isn't now. [Modern tools handle the busywork](/ai-enabled-marketing) that used to require a floor of junior staff, which means fewer people and better tools, with the senior hours pointed at your business instead of at production.

## Four things a small team does that a big firm can't

**1. One senior marketer owns the thread, start to finish.** There's no hand-off from strategist to account manager to whoever's free that week. The person who set the direction is the person who answers for whether it worked. You're not getting junior thinking, and you're not paying for the oversteps that come with it. A senior marketer can also carry more today than a senior marketer could a few years ago, because of what runs underneath them.

**2. Modern tools do the busywork, not the thinking.** Reporting, drafting and first-pass research run in the background. That frees the senior hours for the judgment calls that need a person, rather than for building another deck.

**3. Decisions get made once.** A change to the plan doesn't queue behind three layers of approval before anyone can act on it. You raise it, we decide, it goes live. The speed comes from a shorter distance between the idea and the doing, not from anyone working harder.

**4. The story doesn't change on the way to the invoice.** What you hear in the pitch is what shows up in the room six months later, because it's the same person saying it both times. No senior team for the sale and a junior team for the work.

## Where a bigger shop genuinely wins

I'd be selling you something if I stopped there. There are jobs a small team shouldn't take, and pretending otherwise would be exactly the kind of ego I'm arguing against.

Real scale is the first one. If you're running campaigns across eight markets in five languages at the same time, you need a whole operation behind it, and an operation takes people. Production volume is the second. A large shop with a studio, an edit bay and a bench of specialists can turn out more finished work in a month than a five-person team can, and no amount of modern tooling closes that gap entirely. Round-the-clock coverage across time zones is a third. And if you're inside a large enterprise where procurement needs a vendor who already carries the insurance, the security review and the contract history, that is a real requirement and a small team will slow you down.

If that's your situation, hire the big shop. That's the honest answer.

Most of the businesses I sit down with are not in that situation, and size has less to do with it than people assume. We work with Sir Galloway, a dry cleaner with locations across Greater Miami, and Peixoto, a Miami swim and resort wear label carried by Anthropologie, Revolve and Saks Fifth Avenue. Peixoto sells nationwide. We advertise nationwide for them. What decides the fit isn't headcount, it's how decisions get made. If the people who run the business are still close enough to make the call themselves, and nobody senior is watching brand, budget, channels and creative at the same time, that's the spot we're built for. A bigger org chart isn't buying you more attention there. It's buying you more people to route your question through.

## What happens when we grow

The fair question to ask next is what happens when I get busy. If the whole argument rests on one senior person owning the thread, growth is either the thing that breaks it or the thing that proves it.

So here's how we're building. VBO adds senior operators, never a junior floor. The next person here will have the same mileage I do, work the same way, run the same modern tools, and be able to hold a client end to end instead of routing the work to somebody else. That's a slower way to grow, and it's deliberate. It's the only kind of hire that keeps the rest of this piece true.

That's the distinction I'd want you to hear. This isn't one person for hire with a full calendar. It's a firm with a model, and the model is that the person who owns your account is senior, every time, however many of us there are.

## What to look for instead

If you're weighing a small, founder-led team against a bigger shop, headcount is the wrong thing to compare. Ask these instead.

- Who's going to be in the room in month seven?
- Who's going to pick up your phone calls?
- Who's going to be texting you back?
- How fast does the thinking move, and what actually shows up when it does?

The first three should get you a name, not a title. The last one is about pace, and it's the one that tells you whether the next year feels like progress or like waiting. Those four answers say more about how this goes than any credentials deck will.

You don't have to take my word for any of this. It's a five-minute conversation to find out, and you'll know from the answers whether the person pitching you is the person who'll do the work.
