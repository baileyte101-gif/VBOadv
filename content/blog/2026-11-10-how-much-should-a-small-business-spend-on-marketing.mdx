The question has real published answers, and you should have them before anyone asks you to commit to a number. So the numbers come first. After them comes the part the percent can't settle: what the first dollars buy, and in what order. That choice decides more of the year than the percent does, because ads multiply whatever they land on.

## What the published numbers actually say

The Business Development Bank of Canada publishes a working range: 2 to 5 percent of revenue if you sell to other businesses, 5 to 10 if you sell to consumers. That's a sane place to start, and it's the clearest range a real institution puts its name on.

The number repeated most often says the SBA recommends 7 to 8 percent of revenue, and it's worth clearing up: it doesn't appear anywhere on the SBA's own site. What the SBA actually published, in a 2019 post on its own blog, is that there's no hard rule, alongside two averages from the surveys it cites: a little over 1 percent of revenue on advertising, and 7.9 percent on marketing as a whole. The recommendation everyone repeats is folklore. The averages are real.

The high end belongs to the CMO Survey, which in its 2026 edition found companies putting about 9 percent of revenue into marketing on average, and companies under $10 million in sales reporting about 13. Read it knowing who answers it, though: companies with a senior marketing leader on staff, and its smallest-company figure comes from twenty answers with a very wide spread. An owner-run business with no marketing team almost certainly sits lower.

Turn the percents into dollars. A business doing $2 million a year lands at $40,000 to $100,000 inside the 2-to-5 band, which is roughly $3,300 to $8,300 a month. At $5 million, the same band runs about $8,300 to $21,000 a month. That's arithmetic on the published ranges, not a recommendation.

The numbers need a reader. Ours is specific: an established, independently owned business, fifteen or more years in, maybe a few locations or a storefront online, nobody in-house whose whole job is marketing. And we write for that owner because that's who we work with. Most of what's published answers "a small business" in the abstract, a category wide enough to hold a food truck and a forty-person firm, which is how the same percent gets handed to businesses with nothing else in common.

## The owner nobody gave a number

One Miami business we work with is in its fifth decade: six locations, a name its neighborhoods trust. When its owners sat down with us, they knew their business cold, and they knew the total they could commit to marketing for the year, everything included. What nobody had ever told them was what outside help should cost. Not a range. Not even the folklore.

They held the budget in one pot, fees and media and the website together, which is also how the money actually leaves the account. If you hold yours the same way, keep it. The pot's size is what the published ranges are for, and roughly right is enough, because roughly right is all a benchmark can do.

The decision those owners were really facing wasn't 2 percent against 10. It was whether this would be a three-thousand-dollar-a-month year or a fifteen-thousand-dollar-a-month year, and what either number would actually buy. That second question is the one the percent rules never answer.

## What the money buys at each level

The pot splits three ways, whoever spends it. The foundation comes first: a website a customer can act on, where asking, booking or buying takes a minute; Google listings that are claimed, consistent and answered, because the listing is usually the first thing a local customer sees; and the counting, so every inquiry, call and sale lands somewhere you can see it. Foundation work is mostly built once and then kept up, and it takes the first dollars because everything else either stands on it or falls through it.

Then the running work: paid ads on Google and Meta, to reach new customers or remind others to purchase again; the posts that keep the business visible; and a regular reading of what the money did, in numbers you can check yourself. This is the layer most people picture when they picture marketing. It's also the layer that quietly fails when the foundation under it is missing, and the failure shows up as money, not theory: ad clicks landing on a site that loses them, searchers finding wrong hours, results nobody can prove.

Scaling comes last and is the simplest: more money into the ads the counting proves are paying. Spending more is the whole move, and it only works because of the two levels under it.

Inside the pot, one part pays for the work and the other goes straight to the ad platforms as media. The mix shifts with the level: foundation months are mostly work, scaling months are mostly media. Owners sometimes read the work part as overhead, and it's the opposite, because the work is what decides whether the media comes back.

## Sequence beats percent

Two businesses can commit the same percent and have opposite years. For exactly that reason, we run the order the same way for every client: brand before ads, organic social before paid, and the counting proven before a dollar of ad spend. Ads are multipliers. Send more people to a website that loses them, and you've bought more lost people. The same goes for listings that contradict the site, because more searches just find the contradiction faster. And where nothing is counted, the working ads and the duds look identical in the report, so the working ones get switched off right beside them.

If you've already spent, and you're holding a report full of clicks with no sales you can point to, we've written that diagnosis on its own: [why your marketing isn't working](/insights/why-your-marketing-isnt-working). This piece is for the dollars you haven't committed yet.

The six-location business ran the order all the way through. The plan we agreed before any money moved put the foundation first and held the ads until the front of the house was clean. When the rebuilt foundation went live, the ads hadn't started yet, and inquiries rose roughly 35 percent. The lift belongs to the whole foundation rather than to any one piece of it. Then the ads began. They landed on a site built to take an inquiry, with counting that shows what they bring in, which is the only kind of ad spend we'd ask an owner to fund.

## How to set your number this quarter

Pick the goal first. Give it a number. Then set the yearly pot from the published ranges above, sitting higher in your band when more of the foundation still needs building. Spend it in order: foundation until a stranger can find you, believe what they find, and reach you in a minute; ads after, and only ads whose results land in your counting. If an inquiry can't be counted yet, the ad money waits. It will still be there in a month, and by then you'll be able to see what it does.
