Ask how much a small business should spend on marketing, and the answer usually comes back as a percentage of revenue. Published ranges do exist, and you should know them before anyone asks you to commit to a number. What a percentage can't settle is what the first dollars should buy, and in what order. That second decision shapes more of the year than the percent does, because ads multiply whatever they land on.

## What the published numbers say

The Business Development Bank of Canada publishes a range of 2 to 5 percent of revenue if you sell to other businesses, and 5 to 10 if you sell to consumers. It's a sensible place to start, and it's the clearest range a real institution puts its name on.

The figure repeated most often says the SBA recommends 7 to 8 percent of revenue, but that recommendation doesn't appear anywhere on the SBA's own site. In a 2019 post on its own blog, the SBA says there's no hard rule. What that post does cite is two survey averages: a little over 1 percent of revenue on advertising, and 7.9 percent on marketing as a whole. So the recommendation everyone repeats is folklore, while those averages are real.

The high end belongs to the CMO Survey, whose 2026 edition found companies putting about 9 percent of revenue into marketing on average, and companies under $10 million in sales reporting about 13. Before leaning on that, it helps to know who answers the survey. Its respondents are companies with a senior marketing leader on staff, and the smallest-company figure rests on twenty answers with a very wide spread. An owner-run business with no marketing team almost certainly sits lower.

In dollars, a business doing $2 million a year lands at $40,000 to $100,000 inside the 2 to 5 percent band, which is roughly $3,300 to $8,300 a month. At $5 million, the same band runs about $8,300 to $21,000 a month. That's arithmetic on the published ranges, not a recommendation.

Those numbers need a reader, and ours is specific: an established, independently owned business, fifteen or more years in, maybe with a few locations or a storefront online, and nobody in-house whose whole job is marketing. Because that's who we work with, it's who we write for. Most of what's published answers for "a small business" in the abstract, a category wide enough to hold a food truck and a forty-person firm. That's how the same percent ends up handed to businesses with nothing else in common.

## The owner nobody gave a number

One of our clients is a Miami business in its fifth decade, with six locations and a name its neighborhoods trust. When its owners sat down with us, they knew their business cold, and they knew the total they could commit to marketing for the year, everything included. What nobody had ever given them was a number for what outside help should cost, not even the folklore one.

They held the budget in one pot, with fees, media and the website together, which is also how the money actually leaves the account. If you hold yours the same way, keep it. The size of the pot is what the published ranges are for, and roughly right is enough, because roughly right is all a benchmark can do.

The real decision in front of those owners was whether this would be a three-thousand-dollar-a-month year or a fifteen-thousand-dollar-a-month year, and what either number would actually buy. No percent rule answers that second part, and it's the part that decides how the year goes.

## What the money buys

Whoever spends it, the pot splits three ways, and the foundation comes first. It starts with a website a customer can act on, where asking, booking or buying takes a minute. Then come Google listings that are claimed, consistent and answered, because the listing is usually the first thing a local customer sees. The counting rounds it out, so every inquiry, call and sale lands somewhere you can see it. The foundation is mostly built once and then kept up, and it takes the first dollars because everything else either stands on it or falls through it.

Next comes the running work. That means paid ads on Google and Meta, to reach new customers or remind others to purchase again. Alongside them sit the posts that keep the business visible and a regular reading of what the money did, in numbers you can check yourself. This is the layer most people picture when they picture marketing, and it's also the layer that quietly fails when the foundation under it is missing. When it fails, the cost shows up in money: ad clicks landing on a site that loses them, searchers finding the wrong hours, and results nobody can prove.

Scaling comes last and is the simplest of the three, because it's just more money into the ads the counting proves are paying. Inside the pot, some of the money pays for the work itself and the rest goes straight to the ad platforms as media. Foundation months lean toward the first and scaling months toward the second, and owners sometimes read the first part as overhead, when it's what decides whether the media comes back.

## Sequence beats percent

Two businesses can commit the same percent and have opposite years. For that reason, we run the order the same way for every client: brand before ads, organic social before paid, and the counting proven before a dollar of ad spend. Ads are multipliers. Send more people to a website that loses them, and you've bought more lost people, while listings that contradict the site just let more searches find the contradiction faster. Where nothing is counted, the ads that work and the duds look identical in the report, so the good ones get switched off right beside them.

If you've already spent, and you're holding a report full of clicks with no sales you can point to, we've written that diagnosis on its own: [why your marketing isn't working](/insights/why-your-marketing-isnt-working). This piece is for the dollars you haven't committed yet.

The six-location business ran the order all the way through. The plan we agreed before any money moved put the foundation first and held the ads until the front of the house was clean. When the rebuilt foundation went live, the ads hadn't started yet, and inquiries rose roughly 35 percent. That lift belongs to the whole foundation rather than to any one piece of it.

Then the ads began, landing on a site built to take an inquiry, with counting in place to show what they bring in. That's the only kind of ad spend we'd ask an owner to fund.

## How to set your marketing budget

Pick the goal first and give it a number. Then set the yearly pot from the published ranges above, sitting higher in your band when more of the foundation still needs building, and spend it in order. The foundation comes first, until a stranger can find you, believe what they find and reach you in a minute, and the ads come after, but only ads whose results land in your counting. If an inquiry can't be counted yet, the ad money waits, and it will still be there in a month, when you'll be able to see what it does.
