Search for a local business by name, and before its website ever loads, Google hands you its listing: the stars, the hours, the photos, a phone number and a button for directions. For plenty of customers, that listing is the whole first impression. They call from it, get directions from it and judge the business by it, without ever needing the website.

With one store, the listing mostly takes care of itself. The owner walks past the real front door every morning, and when the hours online are wrong, somebody says so at the counter. Add more stores and that loop breaks, so the listings start to drift in predictable ways that cost real inquiries. A customer who reads the wrong hours drives to a dark store, and a searcher who meets a two-year-old complaint nobody answered calls somewhere else.

None of that takes anyone failing at a job, because all it takes is listings that nobody owns. The fixes aren't complicated, and most of them need no marketing help at all, only somebody who owns the job. To make them easy to run on your own business, we've written down the checklist we use.

It's meant for the owner of two to twelve locations rather than fifty, and it isn't a dashboard manual for Google Business Profile. Google's own help pages already explain which buttons to press. What they never tell you is which of your listings is quietly costing you inquiries, or what order to fix things in.

## Why listings break when a business grows

Each location tends to get set up by whoever opened it, often years apart, and from there the details drift. A name gets written three ways, hours outlive a schedule change, and the photos date from two renovations ago.

Google doesn't wait for an owner, either. According to its own help pages, Google updates listings using what users and other sources report, and a suggested edit can go live automatically if nobody responds. We've watched it happen on listings we manage, where a single week brought dozens of service entries nobody at the business wrote, each announced after the fact as "changed based on feedback from Google users or other sources."

Some of what Google adds is harmless, but some of it is expensive. A wrong accessibility label sends a customer with a wheelchair to a door they can't use, and a wrong closing time teaches a neighborhood you're shut when you're open.

Reviews drift fastest of all, because answering them belongs to nobody. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found about three in four people only weigh reviews from the last three months. So years of earned reputation can lose a searcher to one recent complaint left sitting there, and a listing can keep telling an old story long after the business has moved past it.

## How to manage multiple Google Business Profiles

This is the checklist we use. Pull up your own listings and go down it one location at a time.

1. **Claim every location.** Open each listing and look for the words "Own this business?" If they're there, nobody at your company holds the keys, which means you won't see what Google changes and you can't answer a single review. Claiming is free.
2. **One exact name, phone and hours per location, matching your website.** Every mismatch, even a small one, is a customer deciding which version of you to believe, and some of them will believe the wrong one.
3. **At least ten real photos per listing.** Show your counter, your people and your actual storefront, because a listing holding three photos from years ago reads as abandoned, and you're rarely there to explain otherwise.
4. **Answer the recent reviews, aiming at more than half.** Start with the newest and move backward, since those are the ones doing the deciding, and write each answer fresh. The same BrightLocal survey found about half of people discount a copy-and-paste reply. An unanswered complaint reads like an uncontested one.
5. **Flag any location under 4.3 stars for a closer look.** When one store sits well below its siblings, the store itself may not be the problem. A location with only a few reviews can be dragged down by a handful of loud, unanswered stories while its quiet, happy customers go uncounted. Find yours, answer them first, and ask the counter staff what actually happened.
6. **Then read each listing the way a customer does.** Search the store's name from your phone, signed out, and look at what they see. Fix what reads wrong, and give the whole job a name and a repeat date, because a listing fixed once doesn't stay fixed. Google keeps editing, reviewers keep writing, and a correction you submit isn't instantly live, since some of ours took days to appear.

None of the list needs a marketing budget, and all of it needs an owner.
