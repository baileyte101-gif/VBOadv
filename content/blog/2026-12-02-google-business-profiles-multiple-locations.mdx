When a Miami business four decades in asked us to look at its marketing, we started where its customers start: the six Google listings that come up when you search its name. One store was filed under another store's neighborhood, and the descriptions still promised an old version of the business's own service. Nearly two hundred reviews across the six held a strong average, yet the newest sat unanswered, and the newest are the ones a customer actually reads. The replies that did exist repeated one apology, word for word, across years and stores, and one still carried a manager's personal cell number.

None of that happened because somebody failed at a job. The listings had simply never had an owner. For most of the business's forty years that was fine, because the stores had the neighborhood's trust and the phones kept ringing. Then the way customers find a business moved onto those listings, and the listings belonged to no one.

Past one location, Google listings break in predictable ways, and the breaks cost real inquiries: the customer who reads the wrong hours and drives to a dark store, the searcher who meets a two-year-old complaint nobody answered and calls somewhere else. The fixes are not complicated, and most of them need no marketing help at all, just somebody who owns the job. So this piece hands you the checklist we used to rebuild those six listings, written so you can run it on your own business.

It is written for the owner of two to twelve locations, not fifty, and it is not a dashboard manual for Google Business Profile. Google's own help pages explain which buttons to press. What they never tell you is which of your listings is quietly costing you inquiries, or what order to fix things in.

## Why listings break when a business grows

To the person searching, your listing is the whole first impression. They type the name, and before your website ever loads, Google hands them stars, hours, photos, a phone number and a directions button. Plenty of customers never get past that, because they never need to: they call from the listing, they get directions from the listing, and they judge from it.

With one store, the listing mostly takes care of itself. The owner walks past the real front door every morning, and when the hours are wrong online, somebody says so at the counter. With six stores, that loop breaks. Each location was set up by whoever opened it, years apart, and the details drift: a name written three ways, hours that outlived a schedule change, photos from two renovations ago.

Google does not wait for an owner, either. By its own help pages, it updates listings using what users and other sources report, and a suggested edit can go live automatically if nobody responds. We watch it happen on accounts we manage: in a single week, one business's listings gained dozens of service entries nobody there wrote, each announced after the fact as "changed based on feedback from Google users or other sources." Some of what Google adds is harmless. Some is expensive, because a wrong accessibility label sends a customer with a wheelchair to a door they cannot use, and a wrong closing time teaches a neighborhood you're shut when you're open.

Reviews drift fastest of all, because answering them belongs to nobody. BrightLocal's 2026 Local Consumer Review Survey found about three in four people only weigh reviews from the last three months, so ten years of earned reputation can lose a searcher to one recent complaint left sitting there. That was the shape of the six listings we opened: the newer reviews praised the staff by name, the business had clearly earned its current reputation, and the listings were still telling the old story, because nobody was there to tell the new one.

## How to manage multiple Google Business Profiles

This is the checklist we ran on those six listings, and the one we now run on every multi-location business we look at. Pull up your own listings and go down it, one location at a time.

1. **Claim every location.** Open each listing and look for the words "Own this business?" If they're there, nobody at your company holds the keys: you won't see what Google changes, and you can't answer a single review. Claiming is free.
2. **One exact name, phone and hours per location, matching your website.** Not close. Exact. Every mismatch is a customer deciding which version of you to believe, and some of them will believe the wrong one.
3. **At least ten real photos per listing.** Your counter, your people, your actual storefront. A listing holding three photos from years ago reads abandoned, and you are rarely there to explain otherwise.
4. **Answer the recent reviews, aiming at more than half.** Start with the newest and work backward, because those are the ones doing the deciding. Write each answer fresh: the same BrightLocal survey found about half of people discount a copy-and-paste reply, and on the listings we opened, one pasted apology had been doing duty for years. An unanswered complaint reads like an uncontested one.
5. **Flag any location under 4.3 stars for a closer look.** When one store sits well below its siblings, the store itself may not be the problem. On the business above, the gap came down to a handful of loud, unanswered stories dragging the average while the quiet, happy customers went uncounted. Find yours, answer them first, and ask the counter staff what actually happened.
6. **Then read each listing the way a customer does.** Search the store's name from your phone, signed out, and look at what they see. Fix what reads wrong, and give the whole job a name and a repeat date, because a listing fixed once does not stay fixed. Google keeps editing, reviewers keep writing, and a correction you submit is not instantly live: some of ours took days to appear.

That is the whole list. None of it needs a marketing budget, and all of it needs an owner.

## What it changed for the six-location business

For the business whose listings opened this piece, the checklist was one part of a bigger job. We rebuilt the foundation in order: the brand, a new website, the social accounts, and the counting that tells you what every inquiry and call came from. Across the six listings, that meant one consistent brand message matching the new website and social presence, each listing pointing to its own store page, real photos of the real stores, and a plan for the reviews. Only after all of that did the ads start.

When the new foundation went live, inquiries rose roughly 35 percent. We won't hand the listings alone the credit, because the lift belongs to the whole foundation, and that is the point. Listings are rarely a business's only problem. They're the most visible symptom of the real one, which is marketing that nobody inside the business owns.
